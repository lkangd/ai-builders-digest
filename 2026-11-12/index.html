Agent swarms have the potential to drive yet another leap in output from AI. The impact will be a clear separation between teams that adopt this pattern of work and those that don’t.

The progression has roughly been: chat gave us quick turn AI responses that let us work a bit faster, but we were still rate limited by our interactions; then agents that can go off and complete entire tasks for us, accelerating hours or days of work into minutes; to now swarms of agents that are goal seeking and will find any route to the successful completion of a project, where there’s no inherent limit to the productivity gains.

We can imagine a future where, for any task in which value can be created within a self-contained digital environment, swarms will be able to complete the work faster than any other approach.

Essentially any area where value creation is currently bound by the amount of research you can do, the amount of decisions made with a set of software tools, the amount of data processed or consumed, and the amount of code (or code adjacent) that can be generated.

This means swarms will play a big role in game development, coding, trading, life sciences research, IT automation, cyber, math breakthroughs, animation, and plenty of other areas of work that have these characteristics.

There’s a lot of opportunity to build the harnesses, applied AI products, and infrastructure for how these agents operate. And most importantly there’s a ton of opportunity for those that can wire up work in this way. It’s going to be an entire new field of FDE work available.
译文
智能体集群有望推动 AI 产出再次跃升。其影响将是：采用这种工作模式的团队与不采用的团队明显拉开差距。

演进大致是：聊天让我们获得快速的 AI 响应，工作快了一点，但仍受交互次数限制；随后是能独立完成整个任务的智能体，把数小时甚至数天的工作压缩到几分钟；如今则是目标导向的智能体集群，会寻找任何路径成功完成项目，生产力提升本质上没有上限。

可以想象一个未来：只要任务能在自包含的数字环境中创造价值，集群就能比其他任何方式更快完成。

本质上，任何当前价值创造受限于可做多少研究、用一组软件工具能做多少决策、能处理或消费多少数据、能生成多少代码（或代码相关内容）的领域，都是如此。

这意味着集群将在游戏开发、编程、交易、生命科学研究、IT 自动化、网络安全、数学突破、动画以及许多具备这些特征的其他工作领域发挥重要作用。

Quoting
Matt Huang @matthuang
Swarm riders and Swarm-native companies

AI-use even among highly AI-native people is power-law distributed.

There seems to be a new class of people — call them “swarm riders” — who are capable of consistently commanding large swarms of agents to productive ends.

This is a new kind of managerial skill, analogous to the skill it takes to manage large organizations of people, but wholly different in nature and of course more technical.

Based on the swarm riders we know (including many at @Tempo and @Paradigm), these people are capable of productive token spend that is 1000x-10000x higher than median software engineers, and 100x higher than top 5% Claude Code users.

The art of swarm riding is different from software engineering and more akin to machine learning, where practitioners grapple with black box systems: defining reward functions and evals, transmuting non-verifiable problems into verifiable problems, running experiments, etc.

Swarm-native companies are a new class of company founded by and populated by swarm riders. These companies are organized around swarms in a way that conventional companies will struggle to replicate, as the cost of internal human coordination is sand in the gears of an effective swarm. Effective swarm-native companies will define the right reward functions and evals, remove functional and bureaucratic barriers, and then let the swarms cook.

We’ve gotten to see a few swarm-native companies up close — reshaping financial rails @Tempo, building a new gaming platform @Spawn, cyber swarms at Cathedral Systems — and it’s clear these teams wield a significant advantage over pre-AI teams.

Some questions I’ve been wondering about:

1) How fast will swarm riding diffuse through the economy? The implications for token spend could be significant.

2) Can swarms be effectively adopted by existing companies or will it generally track the formation of new swarm-native companies?

3) What becomes scarce as swarm capabilities themselves commoditize (through frontier models becoming better at swarming, swarm capabilities built into harnesses, etc)? Cultures and teams that can define reward functions well and otherwise get out of the way?

BREAKING:

@Google just dropped Gemini 4 Carbon. It’s a much better coder than Argon—but I’m probably keeping Argon for writing.

We’ve been testing it at @every across coding, writing, design, and knowledge work. It can work for hours on an ambitious project, but it doesn’t always know when to stop.

Here’s your day-zero vibe check:

• Coding: On one run of our Senior Engineer benchmark, Carbon scored 44%, versus Argon’s 21%. A big improvement, though still middle of the pack. In a separate test, @hammer_mt gave it a backlog to work through. It identified 6 tasks as ready, then did 15—including work someone else was already doing. He stopped it after almost 4.5 hours.

• Big builds: @RandyCounsman gave it 3D scans of our podcast studio and an equipment list. In one attempt, it built a browser-based model accurate enough to check whether new gear would fit without getting into the shot. He estimates Carbon also wrote about 95% of a big update to Everycut, our internal video editor.

• Code quality: @kieranklaassen values its tests and documentation on large projects, including its record of approaches it considered and rejected. Fable remains his first choice, but Carbon has earned a place alongside it. The catch: It can produce huge files where Fable keeps things more modular.

• Audio and video: Randy found it caught audio editing issues other models missed. Kieran handed it a recorded meeting and asked for shorts. It came back with 6 titled clips.

• Design: @jackcheng got a hyperrealistic ginkgo-leaf app icon with details other models struggle with. Objects and 3D scenes look better than Argon’s. Interfaces split the team: Jack and Randy kept getting boxes within boxes, while @TylerNishida liked its ideas for new interfaces. Kieran says giving it design guidance makes a big difference.

• Writing: Our editorial benchmark put Carbon and Argon about even. If you like Argon, you’ll probably like Carbon. But Carbon costs more, and I haven’t seen enough improvement to justify switching.

My Reach Test: 🟨 I’ll use it sometimes.

Give it one big job with a clear finish line, and review what comes back. It’s also worth trying as a second opinion, especially on audio and video.
