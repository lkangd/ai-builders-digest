• On or about Sept. 30, OpenAI’s technical surveillance team found a surreptitious audio recording device plugged into a USB port on the back of a TV in an executive conference room. It was concealed in toilet paper, installed around Sept. 28, and its contents were encrypted.

Cyber is going to be one of the biggest spaces for AI agents in the future.

Obviously the risks of software exploits and the attack surface area increases 100 fold due to the ability for agents to work through any crack to find what they’re looking for.

But this goes the other way as well. The ability to have agent swarms review every line of code, test any number of permutations of exploits, and research any incident faster than humans as they happen, will be a huge boost in how we secure systems in the future.

Cyber has always been a category where it’s hard to keep up with (and triage) all the incoming risks, alerts, and changes in the enterprise and across all system. You can never hire enough people or work through the backlog of all known risks. Agents actually give you a chance finally to have far better coverage of this.

This is going to be a huge opportunity as well because we’ll see plays at the labs, data providers, the layer of the network and firewall, identity, devices and endpoints, SDLC, and even how we protect our data in the enterprise. Lots of interesting dimensions to cyber at the moment.
译文
网络安全未来将成为 AI 智能体最重要的领域之一。

显然，由于智能体能钻任何空子找到目标，软件漏洞利用风险和攻击面会扩大 100 倍。

但反过来也成立。让智能体集群逐行审查代码、测试任意数量的漏洞利用组合，并在事件发生时比人类更快地调查任何事件，将极大提升我们未来保护系统的能力。

网络安全一直很难跟上并优先处理企业及所有系统中不断涌入的风险、告警和变化。你永远招不够人，也清不完所有已知风险的积压。智能体终于让你有机会大幅提升覆盖。

这也将是一个巨大机会，因为我们会看到在实验室、数据提供商、网络和防火墙层、身份、设备与终端、SDLC，甚至企业数据保护方式上的各种布局。当前网络安全有很多有趣的维度。

At Box, we've been testing Gemini 4 Argon on our Box AI Complex Work eval for the past week. It's a huge upgrade over Gemini 3.1 Pro, jumping by 14 percentage points across all our tests, and firmly at the frontier.

In our evaluation, we give the Box Agent (using Gemini 4 Argon) a variety of complex enterprise tasks involving enterprise documents in a range of industries. These tasks approximate the kind of hard, time-consuming work that knowledge workers do every day with their enterprise content. Gemini 4 is a huge leap on effectively every dimension of this work.

Gemini 4 is far more capable on math, logic, analytics, and deep knowledge of a variety of business domains. It also works far harder to complete tasks fully without stopping short of the desired result. This is incredibly important for complex enterprise workflows that depend on robust task execution.

Here are some example wins that we saw to put the improvements in perspective:

* Financial services (+18 points): a funding-gap analysis for a family-owned company, where the brief spelled out every step of the projected statements, down to the interest on the bank loan and the overdraft for each year. Gemini 4 Argon built them exactly as specified, every line broken out and carried through to the funding gap. Gemini 3.1 Pro folded the interest into a single figure.

* Healthcare (+25 points): an intensive care management plan where both models spotted a dangerous potassium level and a drug interaction. Only Gemini 4 Argon went back and pulled the offending drugs from the final regimen, so its plan actually matched its own diagnosis.

* Technology (+37 points): a product performance review deciding which of two regional markets should have its cloud infrastructure optimized first. Gemini 4 Argon pulled the right month's user, retention and per-user figures for both regions and correctly flagged North America. Gemini 3.1 Pro often pulled the wrong rows, at one point reporting retention above 100%, and pointed the effort at the wrong region.

* Energy (+16 points): an energy consumption report across two facilities that had to flag any gaps in the meter data. Gemini 4 Argon consistently caught that the data center's smart meter log stops in November. Gemini 3.1 Pro often missed it, at times declaring the logs complete.

* Consumer products (+33 points): a product launch evaluation deciding which production expansion to fund. Gemini 4 Argon saw that existing inventory could bridge an early capacity gap, which made the smaller expansion viable with an earlier launch and a faster break-even. Gemini 3.1 Pro defaulted to the larger expansion and the later timeline.

Gemini 4 Argon will be available in the Box AI Studio shortly for anyone to build custom Box Agents with.

Redesigning source control at Anthropic
blog
At Anthropic, coding is no longer the bottleneck to building systems or doing research. Claude is able to autonomously iterate on research loops, micro-optimize complex programs, and build new services from the ground up at a speed and scale we haven’t seen before. The bottleneck, now, is all of the developer infrastructure built for human scale: source control, builds, and CI. In May 2026, Anthropic averaged around 2,500 code changes merged per day; as of September, we merged over 30,000 per day. We don’t expect this rate of growth to slow down anytime soon; the latent demand for merging code is practically infinite, and every bottleneck we remove immediately leads to a further increase in the amount of code we produce. Changes merged to our monorepo per day, from January to September 2026. In order to support this growth, we built Scion, an in-house version control system, and fully migrated our monorepo from git in five months. We also built and shipped a code review surface (called Antique), code search, a rich source control IAM system, novel ways of managing and landing large amounts of reviewed code, and more, all in that same time period. In this post, we’ll focus on how we built Scion specifically. It makes for an interesting case study: Claude’s ability to write code was both what put us in this situation in the first place and how we were able to quickly build a high-quality, scalable system to deal with it. Growing our monorepo Before we explore what we built, we should acknowledge how unusual our workload is. Unsurprisingly, Anthropic uses Claude to write software far more than almost any other company, so we’re often the first to see where tools built for human scale start to strain. Source control is just one example of this. While we don’t expect many organizations to have these problems in the very near term, we suspect it won’t be too long before others who use AI coding tools experience these same pressure points, which is why we wanted to share what we built and what we learned. Git is a decentralized system: every clone is a complete, self-contained repository that can compute diffs, read history and blame, and check out any revision while fully offline. For the vast majority of repositories, this is the right design, and it’s a large part of why git became the standard. As our monorepo grows, it increasingly doesn’t fit that design space. The history of our main branch alone is almost 100 GB, and a compressed working copy is over 25 GB. We perform on the order of 25 million code checkouts per day for CI, before counting developer environments, agent sandboxes, and research jobs. Fetched naively, that would be over 600 PB of git traffic per day. Git offers tools for working with a slice of a repository, such as shallow clones, sparse checkouts, and promisor remotes. Beyond these, there are standard workarounds for large monorepos, like internal git mirrors and repository snapshots (seeds) kept in blob storage. We’ve used all of them—at our peak, we operated several hundred internal git mirrors to deal with the load. This bought us time, but they became harder to operate as our merge rate increased. Seeds fell further from the tip of main between refreshes, and it took more and more care to keep operations that depend on history, like diff and blame, working on partial clones. The typical setup for scaling git checkouts. Taking a step back, we noticed that, like most companies, we were using git as a centralized system with one authoritative copy, but nearly all of our clients were agents and jobs that are online by definition. The properties that make git excellent for distributed, offline work were actively undermining our ability to scale as the repo grew. So we asked ourselves: if we designed source control with the fact that agents are always online in mind from the start, what would it look like?
