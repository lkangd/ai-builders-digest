Weekend hack: HackerTube. I was annoyed with YouTube's recommendations so I had Claude filter down 43M comments over 20 years of HN to 6,454 videos that keep being recommended. *Significantly* better viewing experience, ~2 hours with Opus 5.5:

- Underlying data from the comment analysis as one zip file, point your Claude at it and ask for your own version
- HackerTube Longs ✊

I use Claude Code (desktop app for Mac), Opus 5.5 on Medium effort as my default. Once the box was up I gave Claude the IP and told it update, secure, harden, deploy and optimize the code, and it did the whole thing.

Many awesome projects that would have normally taken weeks/months even can now be few-shotted for breakfast. What else are we not thinking of.

Peter Yang @petergyang
I had a great chat with my friend @jacalulu (PM Director at Google Labs) about how Gemini 4 changed how Google builds products and how parents can get kids to explore AI.

Some takes I loved:

1. “It shouldn’t take someone longer to read your doc than it took you to write it.”

Writing is thinking. AI can be a thought partner, but you should firmly be in the driver’s seat.

2. “It’s no longer about can you build it, it’s about should it even exist?”

Code went from a scarce asset to basically free. If you can “throw a couple of agents at it over a weekend,” it’s probably not a durable product. So the hard part now is deciding what’s worth building in the first place.

3. “Why do you even want a person in your product?”

Agents do more of our work every month, so you should ask if your product needs a UI at all. Jaclyn thinks people will still show up to create, learn, or be entertained. Any task that feels like a chore should be built for agents first.

4. “Don’t just give users exactly what they want.”

Talk to them all the time, but find the root problem and solve it in a way they wouldn’t have thought of. You want them to walk away thinking, “I didn’t think this was possible, but it’s exactly what I wanted.”

5. “You shouldn’t expect users to know how to write elaborate prompts.”

Small business owners don’t have time to start from an empty prompt box. So her team built Pomelli, Google’s tool for SMBs to create on-brand content, to read your website and learn your business first.

6. “Let your kid be your creative director.”

Jaclyn’s 5-year-old loves scary stories, so they made their own podcast, Maya and the Fog. Her daughter picks the voices, names the characters, and gives notes on each cut, and Jaclyn has the agent produce the episode. It’s live on Spotify.

Patrick Collison @patrickc
I'm now building an "improvements" feature into all of my personal vibecoded applications: a freeform text input that's hooked up to an agent that yolo implements whatever is submitted.

It's an interesting experience -- the activation energy barrier to sanding any rough edge or implementing any new feature becomes extremely low. Someone else who uses one of the applications I created has already implemented 150 improvements over the course of a few days. This pattern feels like a new kind of tool -- liquid software? -- and every application that doesn't have this feature now has a slightly rigid and anachronistic flavor. Why can't I just tell it how I want it to be different?

Now, it's not obvious to me how this pattern can or should scale. Does it become unmaintainable after a while? (Maybe model intelligence improvement outstrips my feature appetite such that it's fine?) How does collaborative software work in this model? Can software designed this way be secure and reliable?

Whatever the larger questions about broader deployment, it definitely works great in a personal context. It reminds me a little of the visions of Lisp/Lisp machines, Smalltalk, and HyperCard -- systems that the user can take control of and modify. Those visions never quite worked, though: the vast majority of software today is not end-user modifiable in any useful way. So, while this "liquid" affordance is a very small touch, the way it changes one's relation to the software feels a bit transgressive and empowering in a Lickliderian and Engelbartian way.

Increasingly this will be the new job description for a lot of roles, not just coding. We’re clearly moving from a world where agents help us do our work incrementally better or faster, to us helping agents do their work.

This turns everyone in one of these positions to that of a manager of a team of agents, instead of an individual contributor with just some added leverage from AI. The biggest areas of impact will be information-heavy jobs, like coding, cybersecurity, legal work, biotech, engineering, and many other fields.

And as a result, in these jobs your leverage will begin to be decoupled from the normal ratio of hours spent working to output generated. For essentially the history of knowledge work the main variable you had to play with was hours of work done (assuming fixed level talent/coordination/strategy).

Now we’ve separated these variables because agents can parallelize work for us, to in theory execute as much as we need to pull off whatever goal we’re trying to accomplish. The people and organizations that discover this sooner, and orient their operations around it, will see compounding gains in their workflows.
译文
越来越常见的是，这将成为许多岗位的新职位描述，而不只是编程。我们显然正从一个智能体帮我们把工作逐步做得更好或更快的世界，转向我们帮智能体做它们的工作。

这会让身处这些岗位的每个人，从只是借助 AI 获得一些杠杆的个体贡献者，变成一支智能体团队的管理者。影响最大的将是信息密集型工作，如编程、网络安全、法律工作、生物科技、工程以及许多其他领域。

因此，在这些工作中，你的杠杆将开始与通常的“投入工作时间与产出之比”脱钩。几乎在整个知识工作史上，你能调节的主要变量就是工作时长（假设才能/协调/战略水平固定）。

现在我们把这些变量分开了，因为智能体可以为我们并行处理工作，理论上能执行足够多的工作来实现我们想达成的任何目标。越早发现这一点并据此调整运营的人和组织，将在其工作流中看到复利式收益。

Quoting
Freda Duan @FredaDuan
10 Spicy Takes After Talking to 50 Friends at the Frontier of Robotics 🤖🔥

1/ Astra IS robotics' GPT-3 moment. Ironically, now that it's finally here, some in robotics seem reluctant to acknowledge it. Perhaps because it (sadly) didn't come from a robotics company?

2/ Robotics is becoming just another vertical for frontier labs. Not so different from coding in some respects. Why reinvent general intelligence for every industry?

3/ Robotics was too hard to underwrite until now. No convincing pretraining scaling law, no convergence on models, data, or hardware. No wonder some critics dismissed it as a fragmented industry playing an expensive "demo game". Astra may finally offer a credible path to scale.

4/ Robotics foundation-model startups have a case. But how strong is it? The argument: Astra handles System 2 (reasoning), while specialized models retain System 1 (skills) and System 0 (motor control). Fair. But isn't that already a narrower value proposition? And who says the boundary stops there?

5/ Follow the talent. Word is OpenAI reorganized its 30–40-person robotics team around the core Astra model. Hearing similar moves toward general models at @Tesla_Optimus. Org charts may tell us more than any demo.

6/ The robotics data gold rush is real. Million-hour procurement programs, some potentially reaching tens of millions of hours. Talk of budgets approaching $1B per frontier lab.

7/ Bullish on robotics hardware volumes. As intelligence becomes more accessible, the barriers to building useful robots could fall dramatically. The moat shifts toward reliability, dexterity, integration, and deployment.

8/ Robotics could fuel the next semiconductor and memory supercycle. A 100B-parameter model running on the edge requires ~50GB just for 4-bit weights. Now multiply that by millions of robots. Why do you think @JensenHuang is so obsessed with robotics? @elonmusk saw it coming: https://x.com/elonmusk/status/2114772074438988256?a=ZJpCuCM8Iv8RkRR_g_A&s=20

9/ Is self-driving immune to the general-model threat? After coding and robotics, why assume self-driving remains the exclusive domain of proprietary, vertical models? Robotaxi increasingly feels like a solved problem to me. But if and when frontier labs move into self-driving, we could see an Apple vs. Android dynamic. Think about the implications for $TSLA, Waymo, and other OEMs.

10/ The next bottleneck may not be intelligence. It's deployment. Models are improving faster than our ability to deploy robots in the messy real world. Integration, hardware variance, failure recovery, and economics remain brutally hard. The real moat may be turning intelligence into reliable, profitable physical work.

--
I realize some of these takes may rub people the wrong way, especially those who have spent years doing the hard work of advancing robotics. That's not my intention.

I'm sharing them to spark debate, not to declare winners and losers. Astra has only been out for a month or so, and it's far too early to draw definitive conclusions. More importantly, when the TAM is this enormous - potentially the largest in tech - there should be plenty of room for different approaches and many winners.
